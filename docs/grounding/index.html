<h1>How grounding works</h1> <p>The claim ledger is the only factual substrate the writer is given. Everything else in <code>draft</code> exists to make that ledger trustworthy.</p>

The gate

<p>Each source section is mined for claims. A claim is a short factual statement plus the span of source text that supports it. <code>Verify</code> applies nine checks in order and returns the first failure as its reason:</p> <ol> <li>The claim and its quote are both non-empty.</li> <li>The quote is at least 12 runes long.</li> <li>The quote is valid UTF-8.</li> <li>The quote holds no replacement character.</li> <li>The quote occurs in the source.</li> <li>The quote does not end mid-clause.</li> <li>The claim type is one of the accepted kinds.</li> <li>The claim strength is one of the accepted values.</li> <li><strong>Every numeric token in the claim also appears in the quote.</strong></li> </ol> <p>Anything else is dropped and counted. A thin source visibly yields a thin ledger rather than a padded one.</p>

Why checks 3 and 4 exist

<p>Quote matching normalises both sides — lowercased, whitespace collapsed, smart quotes folded — so a sentence wrapped across lines or split by a column gutter still verifies. That tolerance is necessary: PDF text extraction produces both constantly, and byte-exact matching would drop true claims from every real paper.</p> <p>But normalisation is lossy in one dangerous way. <code>strings.ToLower</code> maps every invalid UTF-8 byte to the replacement character, so two <em>different</em> invalid byte sequences normalise to the same text — and a fabricated quote could match a source it does not occur in. A fuzzer found it. Requiring the quote to be valid UTF-8 and free of replacement characters closes it, because normalisation can only ever <em>introduce</em> that character, never any other.</p> <p>Whitespace collapsing carries no equivalent risk: a run of spaces collapses to one, never to none, so <code>a b</code> can never match <code>ab</code>.</p>

After the writing

<p>A finished draft is checked twice more. The house rules cover structure, the word band, banned vocabulary and emoji. The faithfulness pass cross-checks the article against the ledger that grounded it — flagging a metric term no claim supports, an ending that does not close a sentence, and near-duplicate paragraphs, with ungrounded numbers and over-stated hedges reported as warnings.</p> <p>A violation triggers a targeted rewrite, not a shrug.</p>

Resume cannot weaken this

<p><code>--resume</code> reuses a ledger from an earlier attempt, but it re-verifies every record against the freshly re-read sources first. A resumed ledger is trusted because it still passes the same gate, not because <code>draft</code> wrote it. Edit a source underneath and the records it no longer supports are dropped.</p>
